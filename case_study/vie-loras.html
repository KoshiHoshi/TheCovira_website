Growth Case: Scaling Vie Loras

From Market Opportunity to Sustainable Growth

Back

Every successful business begins with a belief.

For Vie Loras, that belief was simple: the fragrance industry had a significant gap between what customers wanted and what most brands were offering.

Rather than immediately investing heavily into inventory, branding, and customer acquisition, the team took a different approach. The goal was not simply to launch a perfume brand. The goal was to understand the market deeply enough to build a product people would genuinely return to.

The journey began with extensive field research focused on customer preferences, buying behavior, pricing sensitivity, fragrance selection patterns, and market positioning. Conversations with potential customers, competitor analysis, and purchasing trend observations all helped shape the first version of the product.

Only after gathering enough market intelligence was the MVP launched.

The objective at this stage was not scale.

The objective was validation.

Phase 1: Understanding Product-Market Fit

The first months of growth were dedicated to learning.

Customer acquisition campaigns were launched through Meta with a daily budget of approximately ₹2,000.

Early performance was highly encouraging.

  • ROAS reached as high as 13
  • RTO remained around 9%
  • Customer acquisition costs were extremely efficient

On paper, the business appeared to be performing exceptionally well.

However, growth metrics alone do not create long-term businesses.

Instead of celebrating early results, the focus shifted toward understanding customer behavior.

Why were customers buying? Why were some customers returning? Why were others not? What products were creating the strongest first impressions?

The analysis revealed an important pattern.

A significant percentage of repeat customers entered the ecosystem through a ₹500 tester pack.

This was a critical insight.

The tester pack reduced purchasing risk, allowed customers to experience multiple fragrances, and created an easier entry point into the brand.

The team now had its first meaningful signal of product-market fit.

Rather than rushing into aggressive scale, resources were allocated toward experimentation.

Approximately ₹15,000 per week was invested into A/B testing.

Multiple variables were tested:

  • Creative formats
  • Product positioning
  • Landing page experiences
  • Messaging angles
  • Offer structures
  • Customer journeys

Every experiment generated data. Every data point reduced uncertainty.

The objective was simple: Build confidence before scaling.

Phase 2: Scaling What Worked

As stronger product-market fit signals emerged, the company entered its first scaling phase.

Advertising investment increased to approximately ₹10,000 per day.

The challenge was no longer acquiring customers.

The challenge was maintaining efficiency while increasing volume.

Performance remained healthy:

  • ROAS between 4 and 5
  • RTO between 8% and 9%
  • Zero cancellations for six consecutive weeks

The consistency of these results reinforced confidence in the underlying growth engine.

As momentum increased, daily spend was expanded further to ₹20,000–25,000.

Despite the larger scale, key metrics remained strong:

  • ROAS between 6 and 8
  • RTO around 11%
  • Cancellation rates around 4%

At this stage, the business appeared highly scalable. Customer acquisition was predictable. Demand was increasing. Sales volume continued to grow.

The data suggested that the business was ready for its next phase.

Phase 3: When Growth Became the Problem

Many businesses fail because they cannot generate growth.

Others fail because they scale faster than their systems can support.

Vie Loras encountered the second challenge.

Encouraged by previous success, advertising spend was rapidly increased to approximately ₹35,000 per day.

Sales crossed ₹2 lakh per day.

From the outside, the business appeared to be thriving. Revenue was growing. Orders were increasing. Advertising dashboards looked healthy.

However, a deeper analysis revealed a dangerous trend.

Reported ROAS and actual profitability were beginning to diverge.

Customer acquisition metrics looked positive. Business economics did not.

The company had unknowingly reached a point where scaling was amplifying inefficiencies. Every weakness inside the funnel was becoming more expensive.

What had previously been small inefficiencies at lower volumes became major profitability leaks at scale.

The business was growing. But it was not growing efficiently.

This realization became a turning point.

Phase 4: Stepping Back to Move Forward

Many businesses respond to declining performance by increasing budgets.

Vie Loras chose a different path.

Instead of pushing harder, growth activities were paused.

For approximately fifteen days:

  • Advertising campaigns were halted
  • Customer journeys were reviewed
  • Conversion funnels were analyzed
  • Website experiences were evaluated
  • Operational workflows were examined

The goal was not optimization.

The goal was diagnosis.

The team wanted to understand exactly where profitability was being lost and why previously successful systems were breaking down.

The audit revealed several bottlenecks throughout the customer journey. Some issues were affecting conversion. Others were affecting customer experience. Several problems were hidden beneath healthy top-level metrics and only became visible when performance was examined end-to-end.

The conclusion was clear.

Incremental fixes would not solve the problem.

The growth system needed to be rebuilt.

Phase 5: Rebuilding the Growth System

The next phase focused on rebuilding rather than patching.

The website was redesigned. Conversion bottlenecks were removed. Landing page experiences were improved. Acquisition funnels were restructured. Customer journeys were simplified. Operational processes were tightened.

Most importantly, success was no longer measured exclusively through advertising dashboards.

The focus shifted toward business metrics:

  • Profitability
  • Customer retention
  • Repeat purchase behavior
  • Customer lifetime value
  • Operational efficiency

This shift fundamentally changed how growth decisions were made.

The team moved from chasing growth metrics to building a sustainable growth engine.

Results

Following the rebuild, performance improved significantly.

  • ROAS increased to 8–9
  • Realised ROAS improved to approximately 5.6
  • Cancellation and RTO rates stabilized around 12%
  • Customer retention exceeded 30%

However, the most important result was not a metric.

It was customer behavior.

Repeat purchases continued to increase without discount coupons, loyalty incentives, retention campaigns, or aggressive promotional offers.

Customers returned because they valued the product.

The strongest indicator of product-market fit was no longer acquisition performance.

It was customer willingness to buy again.

Key Learnings

The journey of Vie Loras reinforced several principles that continue to shape growth strategies today.

Product-Market Fit Comes Before Scale

Scaling uncertainty only makes problems larger. The most valuable investment during the early stages was not advertising spend. It was learning.

Growth Exposes Weaknesses

Scaling does not create problems. It reveals them. Every weakness in a business eventually becomes visible when volume increases.

Metrics Need Context

Strong advertising metrics do not always translate into strong business performance. Growth decisions must be evaluated through the lens of profitability and sustainability.

Systems Drive Sustainable Growth

Long-term growth is rarely the result of a single campaign, advertisement, or tactic. It is the result of interconnected systems working together.

Final Takeaway

The story of Vie Loras is not a story about advertising.

It is a story about validation, experimentation, learning, scaling, auditing, rebuilding, and continuous improvement.

Sustainable growth is not achieved by increasing budgets.

It is achieved by understanding customers, identifying bottlenecks, building stronger systems, and making better decisions as complexity increases.

Those principles continue to influence how growth challenges are approached today.
